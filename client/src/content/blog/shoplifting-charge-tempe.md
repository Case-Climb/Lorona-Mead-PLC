The holiday season is the busiest time of year at Tempe Marketplace, Arizona Mills and the big-box stores along the freeways, and it's also when store security is at its most alert. If you or someone you love has been stopped, cited or arrested for a **shoplifting charge in Tempe**, the most important thing to know is this: it's a criminal matter, but it's often one that can be handled without a lasting conviction, especially for a first offense.

In Arizona, shoplifting is governed by A.R.S. § 13-1805. Most cases involving merchandise worth less than $1,000 are charged as a class 1 misdemeanor, the most serious level of misdemeanor. Higher-value cases, and cases with certain aggravating factors, can be charged as felonies. Many first-time offenders may be eligible for a diversion program that can lead to a dismissal.

Below, we walk through how shoplifting law works in Arizona, the difference between a misdemeanor and a felony charge, what to do about the "civil demand" letter that often shows up in the mail, and what your options may be.

## What Counts as Shoplifting Under A.R.S. § 13-1805

Arizona's shoplifting statute is broader than many people realize. It isn't limited to walking out of a store with an item in your pocket. In general, a person commits shoplifting if, while in a store with merchandise displayed for sale, they knowingly obtain goods with the intent to deprive the store of them by doing things such as:

- Leaving without paying the displayed price
- Charging the purchase to a fictitious person or someone else without authorization
- Paying less than the displayed price, for example by switching price tags
- Moving goods from one container to another (like hiding an item inside another product's box)
- Concealing merchandise

**Concealment matters.** Under Arizona law, knowingly hiding unpurchased merchandise on yourself or another person while still inside the store can create a legal presumption that you intended to steal it. That means you can be charged even if you never made it past the registers.

Self-checkout lanes have also become a common source of shoplifting accusations. A missed scan, a "banana for steak" produce code, or an item left in the bottom of a cart can lead to a stop. Whether the conduct was intentional or an honest mistake is often the central question in these cases.

### Store detention

Arizona law generally allows a merchant or its employee who has reasonable cause to believe someone is shoplifting to detain that person in a reasonable manner and for a reasonable time, usually to question them and wait for police. If you are stopped by loss prevention, stay calm, be polite, and remember you don't have to explain or sign anything. Our guide on [what to do if you're arrested in Arizona](/blog/what-to-do-if-arrested-in-arizona) covers your rights in more detail.

## Misdemeanor vs. Felony Shoplifting in Arizona

The value of the property and the circumstances of the incident largely determine how serious the charge is.

### Misdemeanor shoplifting

Shoplifting property valued at **less than $1,000** is generally a class 1 misdemeanor. In Tempe, these cases are usually handled in Tempe Municipal Court and prosecuted by the City of Tempe. A class 1 misdemeanor can carry up to six months in jail, fines of up to $2,500 plus surcharges, and probation. First-time offenders rarely face the maximum, but the possibility is real, and a conviction creates a criminal record.

### Felony shoplifting

A shoplifting charge can become a felony, typically handled in Maricopa County Superior Court, in situations such as:

- The property is valued at **$1,000 or more** (the felony class generally rises as the value increases)
- The item taken is a **firearm**, regardless of value
- The person used a device, container or tool intended to help shoplift, such as a lined "booster bag" or a tool to remove security tags
- The person has certain prior theft, shoplifting, burglary or robbery offenses within a recent period
- The thefts were part of one continuous episode across multiple stores, or connected to organized retail theft

Felony classifications and sentencing ranges under Arizona law can be complex and depend heavily on the facts and any prior record. If you're unsure which category your case falls into, our article on [felony vs. misdemeanor charges in Arizona](/blog/felony-vs-misdemeanor-arizona) explains the practical differences, and an attorney can review your specific charging documents.

## The Civil Demand Letter: What It Is and What It Isn't

A week or two after a shoplifting stop, many people receive a letter from the store or a law firm representing it demanding payment, sometimes several hundred dollars, even if the merchandise was recovered. This is called a **civil demand letter**, and it confuses a lot of people.

Here's what you should understand:

- **It is separate from the criminal case.** Arizona law allows merchants to pursue civil damages for shoplifting. The letter is a demand related to that potential civil claim, not a court fine.
- **Paying it does not make the criminal charge go away.** Only the prosecutor or the court can resolve the criminal case.
- **Not paying it does not create a criminal penalty.** The store's remedy for nonpayment would be a civil lawsuit, which may or may not ever be filed.
- **What you write back could matter.** Any response that admits wrongdoing could potentially be used in the criminal case.

Whether to pay, negotiate, or ignore a civil demand depends on your situation. It's wise to talk with a defense attorney before you respond or sign anything, so the decision doesn't accidentally hurt your criminal defense.

## First-Offender Diversion: Avoiding a Conviction

For many people facing their first shoplifting charge in Tempe, the best outcome may be **diversion**. Diversion is a program, usually offered at the prosecutor's discretion, that lets an eligible defendant complete certain requirements in exchange for the charge being dismissed.

Shoplifting diversion programs in Arizona commonly involve:

1. Attending a theft-awareness or life-skills class
2. Paying program fees and, in some cases, restitution
3. Staying out of trouble for a set period
4. Completing everything by a deadline

If you complete the program, the charge is typically dismissed. If you don't, the prosecution can resume. Eligibility rules vary by jurisdiction and prosecutor, and diversion is never automatic. Factors such as the value involved, any prior record, and the facts of the incident can affect whether it's offered. An attorney can often help you understand whether you might qualify and, where appropriate, advocate for it.

### When diversion isn't an option

If diversion isn't available, that doesn't mean a conviction is inevitable. Possible defenses can include lack of intent (such as a genuine self-checkout error or absent-mindedness), mistaken identity, problems with surveillance footage, disputes over the value of the property, or issues with how the stop and any statements were handled. In some cases, a charge may be reduced or resolved in a way that limits long-term consequences.

## Why a Shoplifting Conviction Matters More Than You Think

It's tempting to treat a shoplifting citation like a traffic ticket and just plead guilty to get it over with. That can be a costly mistake. Theft offenses are often viewed as "crimes of dishonesty," and a conviction can affect:

- **Employment**, especially in retail, banking, healthcare, education or any job involving cash or trust
- **Professional licenses** and background checks
- **Students**, including ASU students, who may face school conduct consequences or problems with future applications
- **Immigration status** for non-citizens, since some theft offenses can carry serious immigration consequences
- **Future charges**, because prior theft-related offenses can turn a later shoplifting case into a felony

If you already have a past conviction, Arizona offers some relief. You may be able to [have a criminal record set aside](/blog/how-to-get-criminal-record-set-aside-arizona) or, in some situations, [seal your Arizona record](/blog/arizona-record-sealing). But it's usually far better to avoid the conviction in the first place.

## What to Do After a Shoplifting Stop in Tempe

If you've been cited or arrested, a few steps can protect you:

1. **Don't discuss the incident** with store staff, police, or on social media beyond giving basic identifying information.
2. **Keep every document**, including the citation, release paperwork, court date notice and any civil demand letter.
3. **Note your court date.** Missing it can lead to a warrant.
4. **Don't respond to the civil demand** until you've gotten advice.
5. **Write down what happened** while it's fresh, including names, times and whether you used self-checkout. Share these notes only with your attorney.
6. **Talk to a criminal defense attorney** before your first court appearance.
