Black Friday weekend brings big crowds to Scottsdale Fashion Square, Kierland, and the shopping centers along Scottsdale Road. It also brings heavy loss-prevention staffing and an active police presence. If you or someone in your family was arrested and the paperwork says "organized retail theft," you are likely confused and worried. Many people assume they will be cited for shoplifting and are surprised to learn they are facing a felony.

Here is the short answer. **Organized retail theft in Arizona** is a separate crime from ordinary shoplifting, defined in A.R.S. § 13-1819. It generally targets taking merchandise with the intent to resell or trade it, or using a tool or device to help take it, and it is charged as a felony regardless of whether the person has a prior record. That is very different from a typical first-time shoplifting case, which is often a misdemeanor.

The good news is that a charge is not a conviction. Prosecutors must prove each element of the offense, and there are often real questions about intent, evidence, and whether the facts actually fit this statute rather than a lesser one. This article explains how the law generally works so you can make calm, informed decisions about what to do next.

## What Is Organized Retail Theft Under A.R.S. § 13-1819?

Arizona's organized retail theft law was written to address theft rings and resale schemes, not the occasional impulsive pocketing of an item. While the exact wording has been amended over the years, the statute generally covers a person who, alone or with others:

- **Takes merchandise from a store without paying, intending to sell, barter, or trade it** for money or something else of value; or
- **Uses an "artifice, instrument, container, device, or other article"** to help remove merchandise without paying. Common examples prosecutors point to include foil-lined bags meant to defeat security sensors, tools to remove security tags, or modified clothing.

Despite the word "organized," you do not necessarily need to be part of a large crew to be charged. The statute can apply to someone acting alone if the other elements are present. That is one reason these charges catch people off guard.

Because the legislature has revised this statute in recent years, the specific language that applies depends on when the alleged offense happened. An attorney can review the current version and how it fits the facts in your police report.

## How Organized Retail Theft Differs From Shoplifting

Ordinary shoplifting falls under a different statute, A.R.S. § 13-1805. Understanding the difference matters, because it can affect whether you face a misdemeanor or a felony.

### Shoplifting (A.R.S. § 13-1805)

Shoplifting generally involves knowingly taking goods from a store without paying, with the intent to deprive the store of them. The seriousness usually depends on the value of the merchandise and on factors such as prior theft convictions. Many first-time, lower-value shoplifting cases are charged as misdemeanors, and some people may be eligible for diversion programs. Our article on a [shoplifting charge in Tempe](/blog/shoplifting-charge-tempe) walks through how those cases typically unfold.

### Organized Retail Theft (A.R.S. § 13-1819)

Organized retail theft focuses on *why* and *how* the merchandise was taken:

- **Intent to resell or trade.** Prosecutors may point to the quantity of items (for example, many units of the same product), the type of goods (high-resale items like electronics, designer goods, cosmetics, or razors), or messages and online listings.
- **Use of tools or devices.** Bringing a booster bag or a tag-removal tool into a store is treated as evidence of planning.
- **Coordination with others.** Lookouts, getaway drivers, or splitting up roles inside a store can lead to charges against multiple people.

### Why the Distinction Matters

The practical gap is large. A shoplifting case might be resolved as a misdemeanor, while organized retail theft is a felony from the start. Whether the facts truly support the more serious charge is often one of the most important questions in the case.

## Felony Consequences of an Organized Retail Theft Conviction

Under A.R.S. § 13-1819, organized retail theft is a **class 4 felony**. Sentencing in Arizona depends on many factors, including prior felony convictions, whether probation is available, and any aggravating or mitigating circumstances, so it is important to get advice about your specific situation rather than relying on general ranges.

For a broader explanation of how felony classes work, see our guide to [felony vs. misdemeanor in Arizona](/blog/felony-vs-misdemeanor-arizona).

A felony conviction can bring consequences that last long after the case ends, such as:

- Possible prison time or a lengthy term of probation
- Fines, surcharges, and restitution to the store
- A felony record that can appear on background checks for jobs, housing, and professional licenses
- Loss of certain civil rights, including the right to possess firearms
- Potential immigration consequences for non-citizens

Separate from the criminal case, stores sometimes send "civil demand" letters asking for payment. These are a different process from the criminal charge, and it is wise to talk with a lawyer before responding.

## What Happens After a Black Friday Arrest in Scottsdale

Every case is different, but the process often follows a familiar path in Maricopa County.

1. **Detention by loss prevention.** Store staff may stop and hold you until police arrive. They often collect video, receipts, and recovered merchandise.
2. **Police involvement.** Scottsdale police may interview you, review store video, and decide whether to arrest or cite you. You have the right to stay silent and ask for a lawyer.
3. **Booking and initial appearance.** If booked, you will typically see a judge within about a day, who sets release conditions.
4. **Charging decision.** Felony cases are reviewed by the county attorney's office, which decides what charges to file. Sometimes a case begins as one charge and is filed as another.
5. **Court proceedings.** Felony cases move through Maricopa County Superior Court, with steps like arraignment, case management conferences, and negotiations.

If you have not yet been contacted by police, or you are unsure what to say, our guide on [what to do if you're arrested in Arizona](/blog/what-to-do-if-arrested-in-arizona) covers the basics. Above all, avoid explaining yourself to store staff or officers before getting legal advice. Well-meant explanations can be used to try to prove intent.

## Common Defenses and Questions in These Cases

A defense attorney will look closely at the evidence. Depending on the facts, issues may include:

- **Intent to resell.** Was there real evidence you planned to sell or trade the items, or were they for personal use? Quantity alone does not always prove intent.
- **The "device" allegation.** Was a bag or object actually used to defeat security, or was it an ordinary purse or shopping bag?
- **Identification.** Crowded holiday stores and grainy video can lead to mistaken identity, especially when several people are involved.
- **Knowledge and participation.** Being with someone who took merchandise is not the same as agreeing to help them.
- **Valuation and charging.** Whether the facts fit organized retail theft at all, or are better described as shoplifting or another lesser offense.
- **How evidence was gathered.** Questions about detention, searches, and statements can affect what evidence the state can use.

Depending on your history and the facts, outcomes may range from dismissal or reduction to a misdemeanor, to diversion, to a negotiated plea, to trial. No lawyer can promise a particular result, but an early, careful review of the evidence gives you the best chance to understand your options.
