College football Saturdays bring huge crowds to Tempe. Tailgates, packed stadium concourses, long lines for rideshares and late nights on Mill Avenue all mix with heat, rivalry and alcohol. Most people go home with nothing worse than a sunburn. But every season, some fans leave in handcuffs after a shove, a thrown punch or a heated argument that got out of hand. If you or someone you love is now facing an **assault charge in Tempe** after a game day incident, you are not alone, and the situation is often more defensible than it feels right now.

The short answer is this: in Arizona, "assault" covers a wide range of conduct, from an unwanted touch meant to provoke someone to an attack that causes serious injury. Most game day incidents are charged as **simple assault**, a misdemeanor. Some are charged as **aggravated assault**, a felony, because of an injury, a weapon or the person involved, such as a police officer. Which category your case falls into, and whether you have a self-defense claim, makes an enormous difference.

This article explains how Arizona law treats these charges, what usually happens next, and how to protect yourself. It is general information, not legal advice for your specific case.

## How Game Day Incidents Turn Into Assault Charges

Crowded, emotional settings create conditions where small conflicts escalate quickly. Common situations include:

- A pushing match in a crowded stadium section, concourse or exit ramp
- A fight in or outside a bar or restaurant after the game
- An argument between rival fans at a tailgate that turns physical
- A scuffle with event staff, security or a bouncer
- A confrontation with police during crowd control or an arrest for something else

In many of these situations, several people are involved, memories are blurred by alcohol and adrenaline, and officers arrive after the key moments are over. Police may have to decide quickly who started it, and sometimes the person who was defending themselves is the one who gets arrested. That's one reason it's important not to assume the police report tells the whole story.

## Simple Assault Under A.R.S. § 13-1203

Arizona's basic assault statute, A.R.S. § 13-1203, describes three ways a person can commit assault:

1. **Causing physical injury** intentionally, knowingly or recklessly. This is generally charged as a class 1 misdemeanor, the most serious misdemeanor level.
2. **Intentionally placing someone in reasonable fear** of imminent physical injury, for example by swinging at someone or threatening them in a way that makes them believe they're about to be hurt. This is generally a class 2 misdemeanor.
3. **Knowingly touching someone with intent to injure, insult or provoke** them, such as an aggressive shove or a slap. This is generally a class 3 misdemeanor.

Note that a simple assault doesn't require a serious injury, or any injury at all in some cases. A shove in a crowd can qualify if the prosecution can show the required intent.

### Possible penalties for simple assault

Misdemeanor assault can carry jail time, fines, probation, and court-ordered classes such as anger management or alcohol counseling. The maximum penalties go up with the class of the offense, and the actual outcome depends heavily on the facts, your prior record and the court. Many first-time defendants are concerned mostly about the long-term effect of a conviction on school, jobs and professional licenses, and those concerns are valid. If you want a clearer picture of how misdemeanors and felonies differ, see our guide to [felony vs. misdemeanor charges in Arizona](/blog/felony-vs-misdemeanor-arizona).

## Aggravated Assault Under A.R.S. § 13-1204

An assault becomes **aggravated assault** under A.R.S. § 13-1204 when certain additional factors are present. Aggravated assault is usually a felony, and depending on the circumstances it can carry a prison sentence. Factors that can elevate an assault include:

- **Serious physical injury**, such as a broken bone, a serious head injury or an injury that causes lasting impairment
- **Use of a deadly weapon or dangerous instrument**, which can include objects like a bottle or other item used in a way that could cause serious injury, not only guns or knives
- **Assault on a peace officer** or certain other protected people while they are performing their duties
- **Assault on someone who is restrained** or whose ability to defend themselves is substantially impaired
- **Entering someone's home** to commit the assault, among other circumstances listed in the statute

On game day, the most common paths to an aggravated assault charge are a punch that causes a serious injury (for example, someone falls and strikes their head), a bottle or other object used in a fight, or contact with an officer during crowd control. The classification and sentencing range depend on which factor applies and whether the case is charged as a "dangerous" offense, so this is an area where specific legal advice really matters.

## Self-Defense in Arizona

Self-defense is one of the most important issues in many game day assault cases. Arizona law recognizes that people have the right to protect themselves and others.

### The basic rule

Under A.R.S. § 13-404, a person is generally justified in using physical force when a reasonable person would believe it is immediately necessary to protect against another person's use or attempted use of unlawful physical force. The force used must be reasonable in proportion to the threat. Deadly force is governed by a stricter standard in A.R.S. § 13-405.

### Important limits

- **Words alone are not enough.** Arizona law says force is not justified in response to verbal provocation alone. Trash talk, insults and taunting from a rival fan, however offensive, do not on their own justify a punch.
- **Starting the fight can limit the defense.** If you provoked the other person's use of force, self-defense may not apply unless you clearly tried to withdraw and the other person kept coming.
- **Proportion matters.** Pushing someone away might be reasonable; continuing to hit someone who is already down usually is not.

### Who has to prove it

Once there is some evidence of self-defense, Arizona law (A.R.S. § 13-205) places the burden on the state to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that you did *not* act with justification. That is a significant protection, but it only helps if the evidence of self-defense is identified, preserved and presented well.

### Defense of others

Arizona also allows reasonable force to protect another person in many situations. If you stepped in to protect a friend, family member or stranger who was being attacked, that may be a legal justification.

## Other Defenses and Issues That Often Come Up

Every case is different, but game day assault cases frequently involve:

- **Mistaken identity.** In a crowd of people wearing the same team colors, witnesses can easily point at the wrong person.
- **Unreliable witness accounts.** Alcohol, darkness, noise and loyalty to friends can all affect what witnesses say.
- **Video evidence.** Stadium cameras, bar security systems, rideshare dashcams, police body cameras and fans' phones may have captured the incident. Video can help or hurt, and some of it is overwritten quickly, so moving fast matters.
- **Overcharging.** A minor injury may be described as serious, or an everyday object may be described as a dangerous instrument. The facts may support a lesser charge.
- **Lack of intent.** Accidental contact in a packed crowd is not the same as an intentional assault.

## What Happens After a Tempe Assault Arrest

Misdemeanor assault charges arising within Tempe city limits are typically handled in Tempe Municipal Court. Felony charges, including aggravated assault, are generally prosecuted in Maricopa County Superior Court. In either case, you may be released with a court date, or you may be held until an initial appearance, where a judge sets release conditions. Those conditions often include an order to have no contact with the alleged victim.

If you are a student, you may also face a separate university conduct process. That process has its own rules and timelines, and what you say there could matter for your criminal case, so it is wise to get legal advice before participating.

## Protecting Yourself Right Now

If you've been charged or think you may be, these steps can help:

1. **Don't discuss the incident** with police, the other people involved, or on social media. Politely decline to answer questions without a lawyer.
2. **Don't contact the alleged victim**, especially if the court has ordered no contact. Violating a release condition can create new problems.
3. **Write down what you remember** as soon as possible, including names of witnesses and where cameras might have been.
4. **Save your own evidence**, such as photos of any injuries you suffered, texts, receipts and rideshare records that show your timeline.
5. **Keep every court date.** Missing court can lead to a warrant.
6. **Talk to a defense attorney early**, while video and witness memories are still available.

Even if a conviction happens, Arizona law may allow you to later seek to have it set aside or sealed. That is a question for down the road, but it's worth knowing that options can exist.
