Veterans Day is a time to thank the people who served. It is also a good moment to talk about something many veterans and their families do not know exists until they need it: **veterans court in Maricopa County**. If you are a veteran facing criminal charges in Phoenix or anywhere in the Valley, you may be able to have your case handled in a specialized treatment court built around the challenges that sometimes follow military service.

In short, a veterans treatment court is a supervised program, not a free pass. Eligible veterans agree to follow a structured plan that usually includes treatment, regular court check-ins, testing, and support from VA staff and volunteer veteran mentors. In exchange, the court focuses on recovery and stability rather than only punishment, and successful participants may receive more favorable outcomes than they would in a regular courtroom.

Every court sets its own rules, and those rules change over time. This article explains how veterans court generally works in Maricopa County, who may qualify, how cases get there, and what to expect. It is general information, not legal advice for your situation.

## What Is a Veterans Treatment Court?

A veterans treatment court is a type of "problem-solving" or "specialty" court. These courts grew out of the recognition that some people end up in the criminal justice system because of underlying issues: substance use, post-traumatic stress, traumatic brain injury, depression, or the difficulty of transitioning back to civilian life. For veterans, those issues are sometimes connected to service.

Instead of simply moving a case through the standard process, a veterans court brings together a team that usually includes:

- **A judge** who sees participants frequently and tracks their progress
- **A prosecutor and a defense attorney** who work within the program's framework
- **Probation or supervision officers**
- **VA representatives**, often Veterans Justice Outreach (VJO) specialists, who help connect participants with VA health care and benefits
- **Volunteer veteran mentors** who offer peer support from people who understand military culture

The idea is accountability with support. Participants are expected to show up, be honest, and do the work. The team responds to progress with encouragement and to setbacks with adjustments, which can include sanctions.

## Veterans Court Options in Maricopa County

Maricopa County is large, and criminal cases are handled at different court levels depending on the charge:

- **Felony cases** are generally handled in Maricopa County Superior Court.
- **Misdemeanor cases** are generally handled in municipal (city) courts or county justice courts, depending on where the offense allegedly happened.

If you are unsure which category your charge falls into, our article on [felony vs. misdemeanor charges in Arizona](/blog/felony-vs-misdemeanor-arizona) explains the difference.

Maricopa County Superior Court has operated a veterans court program, and several Valley city courts have offered veterans court calendars for misdemeanor cases as well. However, the details matter: which courts offer a program, what stage of the case it applies to, and what charges it accepts can differ from court to court and can change over time. Some programs work with veterans who are already on probation, while others may come into play earlier in a case. A defense attorney can confirm what is currently available in the specific court handling your charge.

## Who May Be Eligible for Veterans Court

Eligibility is decided by the court and its team, not by the defendant. There is no automatic right to participate. That said, programs commonly look at factors like these:

### Military Service

Most programs require that the person be a veteran or have served in the armed forces. Some programs consider people who are still serving, members of the National Guard or Reserves, or veterans with various types of discharge. Discharge status can affect eligibility for VA services, which can affect how a program works for a particular person, so this is worth discussing early.

### A Connection to Treatment Needs

Veterans courts are designed for people who would benefit from treatment, such as for substance use or mental health conditions. Many programs screen to confirm there is a treatable need and that the participant is willing to engage.

### The Type of Charge

Courts often limit the kinds of offenses they accept. Cases involving serious violence, certain sex offenses, or other specific charges may be excluded or require special approval. Many participants have cases involving offenses like [DUI](/criminal-defense/dui), drug possession, or other charges where treatment can address the root problem. Whether a domestic violence or assault charge can be considered depends on the program and the facts, and the alleged victim's input may be part of the process.

### Criminal History and Residency

Prior record, current supervision status, and whether the person lives in the area can all be considered.

### Willingness to Participate

This one is simple but important. Veterans court asks a lot of participants. The team generally wants someone who is ready to commit to the program for its full length.

## How Cases Get Referred to Veterans Court

There is no single doorway into veterans court. Referrals commonly come from:

1. **Defense attorneys**, who identify a client's veteran status early and raise the possibility with the prosecutor and the court.
2. **Prosecutors**, who may flag cases that appear to fit the program.
3. **Judges**, who may refer a case after learning about a defendant's service.
4. **Probation officers**, especially for veterans already on supervised probation.
5. **VA staff**, who sometimes learn about a veteran's arrest and help connect them to options.

After a referral, there is usually a screening process. That may include reviewing the charge and criminal history, verifying military service, and an assessment of treatment needs. The team then decides whether to accept the person.

One practical tip: **make sure your veteran status is known early.** Veterans are sometimes not asked about their service at booking or their first appearance, and some do not volunteer it. Telling your defense attorney right away gives them time to explore the program before key decisions are made in the case. ## What the Program Involves

Every veterans court has its own requirements, but participants can generally expect some combination of the following.

### Frequent Court Appearances

Instead of appearing in court only occasionally, participants typically attend regular status hearings, especially in the early phases. The judge reviews progress directly with each participant.

### Treatment and Services

Participants usually follow an individualized plan that may include substance use treatment, mental health counseling, PTSD or trauma care, and medical care. Many services are provided or coordinated through the VA. The plan may also address housing, employment, education, or benefits.

### Testing and Supervision

Random drug and alcohol testing is common. Participants may also be supervised by probation and required to follow conditions like curfews or travel restrictions.

### Mentorship

Many programs pair participants with volunteer veteran mentors. For a lot of veterans, talking with someone who has worn the uniform makes it easier to stay committed.

### Phases and Graduation

Programs are often divided into phases. As participants meet goals, requirements may ease, such as fewer court appearances or less frequent testing. Completing the program usually involves a graduation, and the total length is often a year or more, depending on the program and the person's progress.

### Responses to Setbacks

Relapse and mistakes happen, and treatment courts expect that. Responses can range from increased treatment or more frequent check-ins to short jail stays. Serious or repeated violations can lead to removal from the program, after which the case may return to the regular court process.

## Potential Benefits and Things to Weigh

The possible benefits of veterans court depend on the program and the stage of the case. In some situations, successful completion may lead to reduced charges, dismissal, a shorter or modified term of probation, or a more favorable sentence. Beyond the legal outcome, many participants gain access to treatment and VA services they had not used before.

There are also things to think through carefully before agreeing to participate:

- **The time commitment** can be significant and may be longer than a standard sentence would have been.
- **Entering the program may require a guilty plea** or other agreements, depending on how the program is structured. That decision should be made with legal advice.
- **The program may not be the best choice** if you have a strong defense to the charges. Treatment court should be one option weighed against others, not a reason to skip examining the evidence.
