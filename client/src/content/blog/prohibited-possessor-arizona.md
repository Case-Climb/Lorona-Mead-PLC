A **prohibited possessor in Arizona** is someone the law bars from having a firearm or other deadly weapon. Common examples include people with a felony conviction whose gun rights have not been restored, people on probation for a felony or domestic violence offense, and people a court has found to be a danger to themselves or others. If someone in one of these groups knowingly possesses a gun, they can be charged with misconduct involving weapons, which is a felony.

Many people facing this charge did not realize they were still prohibited. Some thought their rights came back automatically when probation ended. Others were in a car or home where a gun belonged to someone else. These details matter a great deal, and they often shape the defense.

This article explains what the law says, who counts as a prohibited possessor, what penalties can look like, common defenses, and how Arizonans can work toward getting their gun rights back. It is general information, not legal advice for your specific case.

## What the Law Says: A.R.S. 13-3101 and 13-3102

Two statutes work together in these cases.

- **A.R.S. § 13-3101** is the definitions section of Arizona's weapons laws. It defines terms like "deadly weapon," "firearm," and "prohibited possessor."
- **A.R.S. § 13-3102** lists the ways a person can commit misconduct involving weapons. One of them is knowingly possessing a deadly weapon or prohibited weapon while being a prohibited possessor.

Possessing a weapon as a prohibited possessor is generally charged as a **class 4 felony**. That puts it well above a misdemeanor, with the possibility of prison, a permanent felony record, and further loss of rights. If you are unsure how felony classes compare, our guide on [felony vs. misdemeanor charges in Arizona](/blog/felony-vs-misdemeanor-arizona) walks through the basics.

### What counts as "possession"?

Arizona law does not require that the gun be in your hand or on your body. Possession can be:

- **Actual possession** – the weapon is on your person, such as in a waistband or pocket.
- **Constructive possession** – you knowingly have control over the weapon or the place where it is, such as a gun in your glove box, nightstand, or backpack.

The word "knowingly" is important. The state generally has to show you knew the weapon was there and had the ability to control it. Simply being near a gun that belongs to someone else is not always enough.

## Who Counts as a Prohibited Possessor in Arizona?

The definition in A.R.S. § 13-3101 includes several groups. In plain terms, a person is generally a prohibited possessor if they:

1. **Have a felony conviction** (or a juvenile felony adjudication) and their right to possess firearms has not been restored.
2. **Are on probation for a felony or a domestic violence offense**, or are on parole, community supervision, work furlough, home arrest, or another form of release. This category applies even to people convicted of a misdemeanor domestic violence offense while they are on probation for it.
3. **Have been found by a court to be a danger to themselves or others**, or persistently or acutely disabled or gravely disabled, under a court order, and their right has not been restored.
4. **Have been found incompetent or guilty except insane** in a criminal case.
5. **Are in the United States without lawful status**, or hold certain nonimmigrant visas, subject to limited exceptions.

### Federal law is a separate layer

Federal law has its own list of people who cannot possess firearms, and it does not always match Arizona's. For example, some domestic violence convictions and certain protective orders can trigger a federal ban even when state law would not. A person can be cleared under Arizona law and still face federal restrictions, so it is wise to get advice that looks at both.

## Common Ways These Charges Come Up

Prohibited possessor cases often start with something unrelated to guns. Typical situations include:

- **Traffic stops** where an officer sees or finds a firearm in the car.
- **Domestic violence calls**, where police ask about weapons in the home. (See our article on a [domestic violence charge and your first court appearance](/blog/domestic-violence-charge-arizona-first-court-appearance) for what happens next in those cases.)
- **Probation searches** or home visits.
- **Search warrants** for drug or other investigations, where a firearm turns up. Prohibited possessor counts are often added alongside [drug charges](/criminal-defense/drug-charges).
- **Background checks** at a gun store or pawn shop that flag someone as prohibited.

Because the weapons charge is often stacked on top of another case, the overall exposure can grow quickly. That is one reason to talk with a lawyer early.

## Possible Penalties and Consequences

Exact penalties depend on the facts, your prior record, whether you were on release when the offense happened, and how the case is charged. In general, a conviction can lead to:

- **Prison or jail time**, or probation in some cases. Arizona's sentencing ranges for a class 4 felony increase with prior felony convictions.
- **A felony conviction** on your record, with all the consequences that brings for jobs, housing, and professional licenses.
- **Forfeiture** of the weapon.
- **A longer period of being prohibited**, which pushes back any chance to restore your rights.
- **Probation violations** if you were already on probation, which can lead to a separate sentence.

Sentencing law is complex and can change, so a lawyer should review your specific history before you make any decisions about a plea.

## Defenses to a Prohibited Possessor Charge

Every case is different, but these are issues a defense attorney will often look at closely:

### You were not actually a prohibited possessor

The state must prove your status. Sometimes records are wrong or outdated. A prior conviction may have been set aside with rights restored, probation may have ended, or a court order may no longer apply. Confirming your legal status is often the first step.

### You did not knowingly possess the weapon

In shared cars, apartments, and homes, it can be unclear whose gun it was or whether you knew it was there. The prosecution must prove knowledge and control, not just proximity.

### The search was unlawful

If police found the weapon through a stop or search that violated your constitutional rights, a judge may suppress (exclude) the evidence. Without the weapon, the case may be weakened significantly.

### The item does not meet the legal definition

Arizona's definitions matter. For example, the statute treats a firearm in permanently inoperable condition differently. Whether an item qualifies as a "deadly weapon" or "firearm" can be a real issue in some cases.

### Mitigation and negotiation

Even when the facts are difficult, an attorney may be able to negotiate for a reduced charge, an alternative sentence, or other outcomes based on your background and circumstances. Veterans may want to ask whether [Veterans Court in Maricopa County](/blog/veterans-court-maricopa-county) is an option. No lawyer can promise a particular result, but careful preparation matters.

## Restoring Gun Rights in Arizona

Many people with past convictions want to know whether they can legally own a gun again. In Arizona, the answer is often yes, but it usually does not happen automatically.

Here is how it generally works:

- **The right to possess a firearm is treated separately.** Even when other civil rights, like voting, are restored after a first felony, the right to possess a firearm often requires its own court process.
- **Waiting periods apply.** People generally must complete their sentence, including probation, and then wait a set period before applying. The length of the wait can depend on the type of offense, and it is typically longer for offenses Arizona classifies as "serious."
- **Some offenses are not eligible.** Convictions for certain dangerous offenses may prevent restoration of firearm rights under Arizona law.
- **A judge decides.** You apply to the court, and the judge considers your record and circumstances.

A set-aside or record sealing can also be part of the picture. Our guides on [how to get a criminal record set aside in Arizona](/blog/how-to-get-criminal-record-set-aside-arizona) and [Arizona record sealing](/blog/arizona-record-sealing) explain those options. Keep in mind that a set-aside alone does not always restore gun rights, and federal law may still apply. Before you buy, borrow, or handle a firearm, get confirmation that your rights have actually been restored. ## What to Do If You Are Charged

If you have been arrested or think you may be investigated:

1. **Do not discuss the gun or your record with police** without a lawyer. You have the right to remain silent.
2. **Do not handle or move any firearms.** Doing so could create new problems.
3. **Gather paperwork** about past convictions, probation, set-asides, or rights restoration.
4. **Follow all release conditions** carefully.
5. **Talk with a criminal defense attorney** as soon as possible.
