If you or someone you love was just arrested on a domestic violence charge in Arizona, the first court date usually comes quickly, often within a day of the arrest. That hearing is called the **initial appearance**. It is short, and it is not a trial. A judge decides whether you can be released and what rules you have to follow while the case is pending.

For most people the most important result of that hearing is the release conditions, and in domestic violence cases those almost always include a **no-contact order**. That order can keep you away from your own home, your partner, and sometimes your children. Breaking it, even by accident or because the other person invited you, can put you back in jail.

This article covers what happens at the first court appearance, how Arizona's "domestic violence" label works under A.R.S. § 13-3601, and what you can do now to protect yourself. It is general information, not legal advice about your particular case.

## How the Domestic Violence Designation Works Under A.R.S. § 13-3601

Many people are surprised to learn that "domestic violence" is usually not a separate crime in Arizona. It is a **designation**, a label attached to an underlying offense because of the relationship between the people involved.

Under A.R.S. § 13-3601, an offense is treated as domestic violence when two things are true:

1. **The charge is one of the listed offenses.** These include common charges such as assault, threatening or intimidating, disorderly conduct, criminal damage, harassment, criminal trespass, and others.
2. **The people involved have a qualifying relationship.** Examples include:
   - Current or former spouses
   - People who live together now or used to live together
   - People who have a child together, or where one is pregnant by the other
   - Certain relatives by blood or marriage, such as parents, grandparents, children, siblings, and in-laws
   - People in a current or past romantic or sexual relationship

So a disorderly conduct charge after a loud argument with a roommate, or a criminal damage charge for breaking a partner's phone, can carry the DV label even if no one was hurt.

### Why the label matters

The DV designation does more than change the wording on your paperwork. It can bring:

- **Stricter release conditions**, especially no-contact orders
- **Required counseling or treatment programs** if you are convicted
- **Firearm consequences**, including possible federal restrictions after a DV conviction, even for a misdemeanor
- **Enhanced penalties for repeat offenses**, since Arizona law allows certain repeat DV offenses to be charged as aggravated domestic violence, a felony
- **Immigration, custody, housing, and employment effects** that can outlast the case itself

If you are unsure whether your charge is a misdemeanor or a felony, our guide on [felony vs. misdemeanor charges in Arizona](/blog/felony-vs-misdemeanor-arizona) explains the difference.

## What Happens at the Initial Appearance

After an arrest, Arizona's court rules generally require that you be brought before a judge within 24 hours. In many cases this happens by video from the jail. The hearing often lasts only a few minutes.

At the initial appearance, the judge typically:

- **Confirms your identity** and tells you what you are charged with
- **Advises you of your rights**, including your right to an attorney
- **Decides whether to appoint a public defender** if you cannot afford a lawyer
- **Sets release conditions**, which is the main event
- **Gives you your next court date**, often an arraignment or a preliminary hearing, depending on the case

You will not be asked to prove your innocence at this hearing, and the evidence will not be argued in detail. That is a good reason to say very little about the facts. Anything you say in court, or on recorded jail phone calls, can later be used by the prosecution. If you have not already, read [what to do if you're arrested in Arizona](/blog/what-to-do-if-arrested-in-arizona) for more on protecting yourself in the first hours.

### The alleged victim may be heard

Arizona gives crime victims the right to be notified and to be heard at release hearings. The person named in the complaint may speak, either in favor of release or against it. Their wishes can influence the judge, but they do not control the outcome.

## Release Conditions in Domestic Violence Cases

The judge's goal at this stage is to make sure you come back to court and that no one is endangered while the case is pending. Depending on the charge and your history, you might be:

- **Released on your own recognizance**, meaning a promise to appear
- **Released with a bond**, meaning money must be posted
- **Released to pretrial services**, which may involve check-ins or monitoring
- **Held without release** in more serious cases

Common conditions in DV cases include:

- No contact with the alleged victim, directly or indirectly
- Staying away from a home, workplace, or school
- No alcohol or drugs, sometimes with testing
- Surrendering firearms or not possessing them
- Electronic monitoring in some cases
- Attending all court dates and obeying all laws

Release conditions are not a finding that you did anything wrong. They are temporary rules, and they can sometimes be changed.

## Understanding the No-Contact Order

The no-contact order is often the hardest part of a domestic violence charge in Arizona, because it can disrupt your entire household overnight.

### What "no contact" usually means

Unless the judge says otherwise, assume it covers **every form of contact**:

- In-person visits, even brief ones
- Phone calls and texts
- Email, social media messages, comments, and "likes"
- Notes or messages passed through friends, family, or children
- Showing up at a place you know the person will be

### The other person cannot waive it

This is where many people get into trouble. Even if the alleged victim calls you, wants you to come home, or says they have "dropped the charges," the order still applies to **you**. In Arizona, the State decides whether to prosecute, not the alleged victim, and only the court can change a release condition. Contact in violation of the order can lead to your release being revoked and possibly new charges.

### Retrieving belongings and handling children

If you were ordered out of your home, you may need a civil standby, where a police officer accompanies you, to collect personal items. If you share children, ask the court or your lawyer how exchanges should be handled so you don't accidentally violate the order.

### Release conditions vs. an order of protection

A no-contact release condition is part of the **criminal case**. An **order of protection**, governed by A.R.S. § 13-3602, is a separate order that a person can request from a court. You could be subject to both at the same time, and each has its own rules and ways to be challenged. Following one does not mean you are in compliance with the other.

## Can Release Conditions Be Changed?

Yes, in some cases. A defense attorney can file a motion asking the court to modify release conditions, for example to allow limited contact, permit you to return home, or adjust monitoring requirements. The judge will usually consider:

- The facts alleged and any history of violence
- The alleged victim's position, which they have a right to share
- Your compliance with the conditions so far
- Practical concerns such as shared children, work, and housing

There is no guarantee a request will be granted, and until a judge signs a new order, the original conditions stay in full effect.

## Mistakes to Avoid After a DV Arrest

- **Contacting the alleged victim** "just to talk," apologize, or work things out
- **Posting about the case** or the other person on social media
- **Discussing the facts on jail calls**, which are typically recorded
- **Assuming the case will go away** because the other person doesn't want to proceed
- **Missing a court date**, which can lead to a warrant
- **Keeping firearms** if the court ordered you to surrender them

## Your Next Steps
