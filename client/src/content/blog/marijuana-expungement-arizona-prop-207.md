If you were arrested, charged, or convicted for a small amount of marijuana in Arizona before the law changed, you may be able to erase that record entirely. **Marijuana expungement in Arizona** became possible when voters passed Proposition 207, the Smart and Safe Arizona Act, in 2020. The law created a petition process, found in A.R.S. § 36-2862, that lets eligible people ask a court to expunge qualifying marijuana records.

Expungement is different from most other record relief in Arizona. It doesn't just add a note to your file or limit who can see it. When a court grants a Prop 207 expungement, the record of the arrest, charge, conviction, or sentence is treated as if it never happened, with some narrow exceptions for law enforcement. For many people in Mesa and across Maricopa County, that can matter a great deal for jobs, housing, professional licenses, and firearm rights.

The short version: if your past case involved only personal possession or use of a limited amount of marijuana, a few plants grown at home, or related paraphernalia, you may well qualify. Below we cover which offenses are eligible, how the petition process works, where to file, and what to expect afterward.

## What Prop 207 Expungement Actually Does

Under A.R.S. § 36-2862, a successful petition leads to a court order that expunges the record. In practical terms, that generally means:

- The court record and related law enforcement records are sealed from public access.
- Your conviction (if there was one) is vacated, and your civil rights, including firearm rights, are restored as to that offense.
- You may generally state that you were never arrested, charged, or convicted of the expunged offense.

That is a stronger remedy than a set-aside or record sealing under Arizona's general statutes. If you have other, non-marijuana convictions, those remedies may still be worth exploring; our guides on [how to get a criminal record set aside in Arizona](/blog/how-to-get-criminal-record-set-aside-arizona) and [Arizona record sealing](/blog/arizona-record-sealing) explain how they work. But for a qualifying marijuana offense, expungement is usually the best available option.

## Which Past Marijuana Offenses Qualify

Prop 207 expungement covers past arrests, charges, adjudications (including juvenile cases), and convictions for conduct that is now legal or close to it. Under A.R.S. § 36-2862, eligible offenses generally include:

1. **Possessing, consuming, or transporting 2.5 ounces or less of marijuana**, of which no more than 12.5 grams was marijuana concentrate.
2. **Possessing, cultivating, or processing up to six marijuana plants** at your primary residence for personal use.
3. **Possessing, using, or transporting paraphernalia** related to the cultivation, manufacture, processing, or consumption of marijuana.

It doesn't matter whether the case was charged as a misdemeanor or a felony. Before Prop 207, Arizona often charged simple marijuana possession as a felony, so many people carry a felony record for conduct that is legal today. If you aren't sure how your case was classified, our explainer on [felony vs. misdemeanor in Arizona](/blog/felony-vs-misdemeanor-arizona) may help.

### What Usually Does Not Qualify

Prop 207 expungement is limited to personal possession, use, and home growing within the amounts above. It generally does **not** cover:

- Sale, transportation for sale, or distribution of marijuana
- Possession of amounts above the 2.5-ounce / 12.5-gram concentrate limits
- More than six plants, or growing somewhere other than your primary residence
- DUI involving marijuana
- Unrelated charges in the same case (such as a weapons or other drug charge)

If your case involved a mix of charges, the marijuana portion may still be eligible even if other counts are not. This is one of the areas where reviewing the actual police report and court file makes a real difference.

## How to File a Petition Under A.R.S. § 36-2862

The process was designed to be accessible, and many people complete it without complications. Here is how it typically works.

### Step 1: Gather Your Case Information

You'll want the case number, the court that handled the case, the date of arrest or charge, and the specific offense. If you don't have this information, you can often get it from the court's records or by requesting a copy of your criminal history. Paperwork from the original case, such as a plea agreement or sentencing order, can be very helpful in showing the amount of marijuana involved.

### Step 2: Complete the Petition

Arizona courts provide a standard petition form for marijuana expungement. The petition asks you to identify the case and the offense and to state that it falls within the eligible categories. Arizona courts have generally not charged a filing fee for these petitions, which removes a common barrier, but confirm the current fees with the court before you file.

### Step 3: File With the Correct Court

Generally, you file in the court where the case was handled. If you were arrested but never charged, the right place to file may be different, so it's worth confirming before you submit. (More on Maricopa County courts below.)

### Step 4: The Prosecutor's Response

After filing, the prosecuting agency is notified and given an opportunity to respond, usually within a short period set by the statute. The prosecutor may agree that you qualify or may object. Importantly, if the prosecutor opposes the petition, the law places the burden on the state to show by clear and convincing evidence that you are not eligible.

### Step 5: The Court's Decision

The judge reviews the petition and any response. Many petitions are decided on the paperwork, though the court can hold a hearing if needed. If granted, the court issues an order expunging the record and directing the relevant agencies to update their files. If denied, the order should explain why, and you may have options, including an appeal. Timelines vary by court and caseload, so plan for the process to take some time.

## Filing Marijuana Expungement in Mesa and Maricopa County

Where your petition goes depends on where your case was heard. For people with Mesa-area cases, the most common courts are:

- **Mesa Municipal Court** – for misdemeanor marijuana or paraphernalia cases charged by the City of Mesa.
- **Maricopa County Justice Courts** – for misdemeanors handled at the county level in the East Valley and elsewhere.
- **Maricopa County Superior Court** – for cases charged as felonies, which included many past marijuana possession cases.
- **Juvenile Court** – for juvenile adjudications, which are also eligible.

Some people had their case start in one court and end in another, for example, a felony charge that was later reduced. Others were arrested in Mesa but charged by a different agency. If you are unsure which court is correct, filing in the wrong place can delay things. A local attorney who knows the Maricopa County court system can confirm the right venue quickly. ## Why Expungement Can Matter So Much

A marijuana record from years ago can still show up in background checks. Clearing it may help with:

- **Employment** – many employers run criminal history checks.
- **Housing** – landlords often screen applicants.
- **Professional licensing** – nursing, real estate, education, and other fields ask about criminal history.
- **Firearm rights** – a felony marijuana conviction can make someone a prohibited possessor. Expungement under Prop 207 restores those rights as to the expunged offense. Our article on [prohibited possessor status in Arizona](/blog/prohibited-possessor-arizona) explains why this matters.
- **Immigration and travel** – this area is complicated, because federal law treats marijuana differently than Arizona does. If you are not a U.S. citizen, talk to an attorney before relying on expungement for immigration purposes.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even with a straightforward process, people run into avoidable problems:

- **Assuming you don't qualify** because the case was a felony. Felony classification alone does not disqualify you.
- **Assuming you do qualify** without checking the amount. If the record shows more than 2.5 ounces or more than 12.5 grams of concentrate, the petition may be denied.
- **Filing in the wrong court**, especially when a case moved between courts.
- **Overlooking other charges** in the same case that will not be expunged and may need a set-aside or sealing instead.
- **Not following up** to confirm agencies have updated their records after an order is granted.
